Artifact 04 · Leadership & Communication Reflection

Communication as a
Technical Leader

A personal reflection on how communication clarity — or the lack of it — shapes whether technical work actually lands, drawn from real friction I've hit as a backend developer working across teams.

Technical Communication Cross-functional Collaboration Requirements Documentation Leadership Reflection Stakeholder Management

Why I'm sharing this

My first three artifacts show research, building, and business-case reasoning. None of them show something recruiters consistently ask about in interviews: how I actually work with other people when a project starts to go sideways. This artifact is a self-assessment of my communication style as a developer who regularly works across technical and non-technical teams.

For a hiring manager, I think this matters because technical skill alone doesn't prevent miscommunication — and miscommunication is what actually derails projects. Being able to name my own communication habits, where they've failed, and what I changed afterward is evidence of the kind of self-awareness that's hard to teach and easy to notice in someone's absence.

Reflection

Why clarity is the job, not just good practice

Working primarily as a backend developer supporting healthcare systems and API integrations, I've seen how much communication shapes whether technical work actually lands. Code can be logically sound and still cause delays or confusion simply because requirements weren't communicated clearly across teams.

"If the trumpet does not sound a clear call, how will anyone know to prepare for battle? ... Unless you speak intelligibly, how will anyone know what you are saying?" — 1 Corinthians 14:8–9

A steady, predictable signal versus one no one can act on with confidence.

That image stuck with me — communication has to be deliberate and legible enough for someone else to know exactly how to respond. In practice, that means not assuming analysts or testers share my technical frame of reference. Instead, I try to walk through why a change matters, what it touches, and what needs to happen next, so the broader team stays pointed at the same goal rather than working from different assumptions.

To keep instructions clear, I layer communication methods — brief stand-up summaries, written ticket updates, and follow-up messages when something needs more clarification — and check for understanding by asking questions rather than assuming alignment.

A real example

When unclear requirements actually cost us time

On a recent project, confusion arose around how a transaction lookup was supposed to behave after a logic change. We'd discussed the requirement, but the mapping details never made it into clear documentation. Different people on the team walked away with different expectations about the new implementation, and it took extra investigation and clarification meetings to get everyone aligned on the correct behavior.

That experience brought to mind the Tower of Babel story in Genesis 11 — a breakdown in shared language brought progress to a halt. The same dynamic shows up in technical teams: when communication isn't clear, development slows and collaboration suffers. Since then, I've made a point of getting requirement changes confirmed in writing and reflected clearly in Jira comments or documentation, so the whole team works from the same source of truth.

Going forward

How I'm working on this

Structure

Lead with problem + outcome

Share structured updates that lay out both the problem and the expected outcome, not just status.

Upfront alignment

Confirm before assuming

Confirm requirement details up front rather than assuming shared understanding across the team.

Translation

Use concrete examples

Explain technical changes to non-technical stakeholders with real examples, not abstractions.

Openness

Invite questions

Encourage two-way communication so teammates feel comfortable asking questions instead of guessing.

Documentation

Write decisions down

Document decisions clearly so future team members can follow the reasoning, not just the outcome.

Value Proposition

This artifact shows I can recognize my own communication habits — including where they've caused real friction — and translate that recognition into concrete, repeatable practices, not just good intentions.

Unique Value

Rather than presenting communication as a soft skill separate from technical work, I treat clarity as part of the engineering deliverable itself — a requirement that's ambiguous in conversation but never gets written down is, in practice, an unfinished requirement.

Relevance

For any hiring manager building or leading a technical team, this shows the kind of self-awareness that reduces rework: someone who notices when communication broke down, understands why, and has already changed their process in response.

Lessons Learned

Clarity has to be designed, not assumed. The transaction lookup confusion didn't happen because anyone was careless — it happened because we relied on a conversation instead of documentation. Structure has to be built in deliberately.

Self-assessment is uncomfortable but useful. Naming a specific moment where my own communication fell short was harder to write than describing a success would have been, but it's the part of this reflection I actually learned something from.
